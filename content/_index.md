Before quantum, my world was cybersecurity. While studying for my Security+ exam, I watched the instructor briefly explain how a quantum algorithm could break the public-key encryption that secures the internet. I was immediately hooked and started learning everything I could about quantum information science.

Cybersecurity is still a large part of my life. Today, I teach weekly Security+ preparation sessions for 10–20 students on behalf of Miners in Cybersecurity. Along with being a CodePath Tech Fellow for 50+ students in Introduction to Computer Science, this has taught me that the best way to understand something is to teach it.

Researching under Dr. Frias has taught me a different lesson: it's okay to be wrong. While working on AI-assisted classical software verification, I have seen my predictions about which approaches would succeed overturned multiple times. Because my assumptions kept proving wrong, I learned to verify them instead, and that habit is what draws me to verification.

All of this has led to my academic goal: a PhD focused on quantum software verification. Claims made by tools and AI in software are hard to trust and require thorough verification. Quantum programs are even harder to trust because quantum states are hard to read without disturbing them, and outputs are probabilistic. Understanding those states takes physics, so I am pairing computer science with planned minors in physics and mathematics. I want quantum programs verified before the world depends on them.
